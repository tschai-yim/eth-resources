## Function Properties (General)

- A function $f: X \to Y$ is:
    - **Injective**: Each output $y \in Y$ is generated by **at most one** input $x \in X$.
    - **Surjective**: Each output $y \in Y$ is generated by **at least one** input $x \in X$.
    - **Bijective**: Both injective and surjective. Each output is generated by **exactly one** input.
- **Inverse Function**: Only **bijective** functions are "undoable" and have an **inverse function** $f^{-1}$ that maps an output $y$ back to its unique input $x$. [^2.48] [^2.49]

## Invertibility and Inverse Matrices

- **Concept**: An **invertible** matrix corresponds to a **bijective** ("undoable") linear transformation. The **inverse matrix** $A^{-1}$ corresponds to the inverse transformation.
- **Bijectivity of Matrix Transformations** $T_A: \mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^m$:
    - **Wide Matrix ($m<n$)**: **Not injective**. "Squeezing" a higher into a lower dimension causes input collisions. [^2.50]
    - **Tall Matrix ($m>n$)**: **Not surjective**. Embedding a lower into a higher dimension cannot fill the entire target space. [^2.51]
    - **Square Matrix ($m=n$)**: **Potentially bijective**. The only case where inversion is possible.

## Characterizing and Using Inverse Matrices

- **Equivalent Conditions for Invertibility**: For a square $m \times m$ matrix $A$: [^2.53]
    1. $T_A$ is **bijective**.
    2. Exists an $m \times m$ matrix $B$ with **$BA=I$**.
    3. Columns of $A$ are **linearly independent**.
- **Definitions**:
    - **Invertible** (or **non-singular**): An $m \times m$ matrix satisfying the above conditions. Otherwise, it is **singular**. [^2.55]
    - **Inverse of A** ($A^{-1}$): The unique matrix $B$ for which $BA=I$. [^2.56] [^2.57]
    - The inverse of a bijective linear transformation is also a linear transformation. [^2.52]
- **Properties and Formulas**:
    - **Inverse of Inverse**: $(A^{-1})^{-1} = A$. [^2.58]
    - **Inverse of Product**: Order is reversed: $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$. [^2.59]
    - **Inverse of Transpose**: Operations commute: $(A^T)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^T$. [^2.60]
    - **Commutativity of Inverse**: For square matrices, if $BA=I$, then $AB=I$ also holds. [^2.54]
    - **Special Cases**:
        - **1x1 Matrix**: $[a]$ is invertible iff $a \neq 0$. Inverse is $[1/a]$.
        - **2x2 Matrix**: $\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}$ is invertible iff **determinant** $ad-bc \neq 0$.
            - $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad-bc} \begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix}$

[^2.48]: **Definition 2.48 (Injective, surjective, and bijective functions).** Let $X, Y$ be sets and $f : X \to Y$ a function.
    (i) $f$ is called injective if for every $y \in Y$, there is at most one $x \in X$ with $f(x)=y$.
    (ii) $f$ is called surjective if for every $y \in Y$, there is at least one $x \in X$ with $f(x)=y$.
    (iii) $f$ is called bijective (undoable) if $f$ is both injective and surjective.
    (iv) The inverse of a bijective function $f$ is the function $f^{-1} : Y \to X$, $y \mapsto$ the unique $x \in X$ such that $f(x)=y$.
[^2.49]: **Fact 2.49 (Bijective functions and their inverses).** If $f:X \to Y$ is bijective, then $f^{-1} : Y \to X$ is also bijective, and $(f^{-1})^{-1} = f$. Moreover, $f^{-1} \circ f = id$ ($f^{-1}$ is undoing $f$) and $f \circ f^{-1} = id$ ($f$ is undoing $f^{-1}$).
[^2.50]: **Lemma 2.50 (Wide matrix transformations are not injective).** Let $T_A: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be a matrix transformation, and $m < n$. Then $T_A$ is not injective (and therefore also not bijective).
[^2.51]: **Lemma 2.51 (Tall matrix transformations are not surjective).** Let $T_A: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be a matrix transformation, and $m > n$. Then $T_A$ is not surjective (and therefore also not bijective).
[^2.53]: **Lemma 2.53 (Bijective matrix transformations).** Let $A$ be an $m \times m$ matrix. The following three statements are equivalent.
    (i) $T_A : \mathbb{R}^m \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is bijective.
    (ii) There is an $m \times m$ matrix $B$ such that $BA=I$.
    (iii) The columns of $A$ are linearly independent.
[^2.55]: **Definition 2.55 (Invertible and singular matrix).** Let $A$ be an $m \times m$ matrix. $A$ is called invertible if there is an $m \times m$ matrix $B$ such that $BA = I$. (By Lemma 2.53 this is the case if and only if $A$ has linearly independent columns.) Otherwise, $A$ is called singular.
[^2.56]: **Observation 2.56 (Alternative definitions of an invertible matrix).** Let $A$ be an $m \times m$ matrix. $A$ is invertible if and only if there is an $m \times m$ matrix $B$ satisfying one (and therefore all) of the following three equivalent conditions.
    (i) $AB=I$.
    (ii) $BA=I$. (This means that $A$ is invertible according to Definition 2.55.)
    (iii) $AB=BA=I$.
    Moreover, the matrix $B$ is unique.
[^2.57]: **Definition 2.57 (Inverse matrix).** Let $A$ be an $m \times m$ matrix. $A$ is invertible if and only if there exists an $m \times m$ matrix $B$ such that $BA = I$ (or $AB=I$, or $AB=BA=I$). In this case, the matrix $B$ is unique and called the inverse of $A$. We denote it by $A^{-1}$.
[^2.52]: **Lemma 2.52 (The inverse of a bijective linear transformation).** Let $T: \mathbb{R}^m \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be a bijective linear transformation. Then its inverse $T^{-1}: \mathbb{R}^m \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is also a linear transformation (and bijective by Fact 2.49).
[^2.58]: **Observation 2.58 (Inverse of the inverse).** Let $A$ be an invertible $m \times m$ matrix. Then $A^{-1}$ is also invertible and $(A^{-1})^{-1}=A$.
[^2.59]: **Lemma 2.59.** Let $A$ and $B$ be invertible $m \times m$ matrices. Then $AB$ is also invertible, and $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1} A^{-1}$.
[^2.60]: **Lemma 2.60.** Let $A$ be an invertible $m \times m$ matrix. Then the transpose $A^T$ is also invertible, and $(A^T)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^T$.
[^2.54]: **Lemma 2.54.** Let $A, B$ be $m \times m$ matrices such that $BA = I$. Then also $AB=I$.
