## Fundamental Properties of Symmetric Matrices

- **Symmetric Matrix**: Matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ where $A = A^\top$.
- **Real Eigenvalues**:
    - Real symmetric matrices **always** have real eigenvalues .[^lem9.2.8]
    - **Consequence**: At least one real eigenvalue $\lambda$ exists .[^cor9.2.9]
- **Orthogonality**:
    - Eigenvectors of **distinct** eigenvalues are strictly **orthogonal** .[^lem9.2.7]
    - *Proof logic*: Compare $\lambda_1 v_1^\top v_2$ vs $\lambda_2 v_1^\top v_2$ using symmetry ($v_1^\top A v_2 = (A v_1)^\top v_2$).
- **Multiplicities**:
    - Algebraic multiplicity = Geometric multiplicity for **all** eigenvalues .[^rem9.2.5]
    - Complete set of eigenvectors exists (no "missing" eigenvectors).
- **Rank**:
    - Rank equals number of **non-zero eigenvalues** (counting repetitions) .[^cor9.2.4]

## The Spectral Theorem & Decomposition

### The Spectral Theorem

- **Statement**: Symmetric $A$ has $n$ real eigenvalues and an **orthonormal basis** of eigenvectors .[^thm9.2.1]
- **Implication**: Always diagonalizable via orthogonal matrix.

### Eigendecomposition (Spectral Decomposition)

- **Matrix Form**: $A = V \Lambda V^\top$ .[^cor9.2.2][^rem9.2.3]
    - $V$: Orthogonal matrix ($V^\top = V^{-1}$, columns are orthonormal eigenvectors).
    - $\Lambda$: Diagonal matrix (entries are eigenvalues).
- **Sum of Rank-1 Matrices**:
    - $A$ as weighted sum of projections onto eigenspaces :[^prop9.2.6]
    $$A = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i v_i v_i^\top$$
    - Each term $\lambda_i v_i v_i^\top$ has rank 1.
- **Geometric Interpretation**: Scaling along orthogonal axes (eigenvectors).
    - Steps: Rotate ($V^\top$) $\to$ Scale ($\Lambda$) $\to$ Rotate back ($V$).

## The Rayleigh Quotient

- **Definition**: For symmetric $A$ and $x \neq 0$:
    $$R(x) = \frac{x^\top A x}{x^\top x}$$
- **Bounds**: Bounded by spectrum of $A$ :[^prop9.2.10]
    - $\lambda_{\min} \le R(x) \le \lambda_{\max}$.
    - Extrema attained at corresponding eigenvectors ($v_{\min}, v_{\max}$).
- **Use**: Characterizing PD/PSD matrices; optimization.

## Positive Definite (PD) & Semidefinite (PSD) Matrices

### Definitions & Characterizations

| Type | Eigenvalue Def. [^def9.2.11] | Quadratic Form Def. [^prop9.2.12] |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Positive Semidefinite (PSD)** | All $\lambda_i \ge 0$ | $x^\top A x \ge 0$ for all $x$ |
| **Positive Definite (PD)** | All $\lambda_i > 0$ | $x^\top A x > 0$ for all $x \neq 0$ |

- **Tips & Checks**:
    - **Diagonal Dominance**: If $|A_{ii}| > \sum_{j \neq i} |A_{ij}|$, matrix often PD/PSD.
    - **Addition**: Sum of two PSD matrices is PSD. Sum of PD and PSD is PD.
    - **Invertibility**: PD matrices always invertible ($0$ not an eigenvalue). PSD matrices may be singular.

### Gram Matrices

- **Definition**: Matrix $G = V^\top V$ (inner products of columns of $V$) .[^def9.2.13]
- **Properties**:
    - Gram matrices are always **Symmetric** and **PSD** .[^prop9.2.15]
    - **Converse**: Every symmetric PSD matrix is a Gram matrix (Universal Property) .[^prop9.2.16]
    - **Terminology**: By abuse of notation, $AA^\top$ is also called a Gram matrix .[^rem9.2.14]
- **Fundamental Eigenvalue Relation**:
    - **Non-zero eigenvalues** of $A^\top A$ ($n \times n$) and $AA^\top$ ($m \times m$) are **identical** .[^prop9.2.15]
    - Both $A^\top A$ and $AA^\top$ are PSD.
    - *Proof Concept*: If $A^\top A v = \lambda v$, then $AA^\top (Av) = \lambda (Av)$ (Eigenvector maps from $v$ to $Av$).

### Cholesky Decomposition

- Every symmetric **PSD** matrix $M$ factors into $M = C^\top C$ where $C$ is upper triangular .[^prop9.2.16]
- Effectively the "square root" of a matrix.
- **Construction**:
    1. Diagonalize $M = V \Lambda V^\top$.
    2. Square root eigenvalues: $\Lambda^{1/2}$.
    3. Form $M = (V \Lambda^{1/2})(V \Lambda^{1/2})^\top$.
    4. Apply QR decomposition to $(V \Lambda^{1/2})^\top$ to obtain triangular form.

[^lem9.2.8]: **Lemma 9.2.8.** Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ be a symmetric matrix and $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$ an eigenvalue of $A$, then $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$.
[^cor9.2.9]: **Corollary 9.2.9.** Every symmetric matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ has a real eigenvalue $\lambda$.
[^lem9.2.7]: **Lemma 9.2.7.** Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ be a symmetric matrix and $\lambda_1 \ne \lambda_2 \in \mathbb{R}$ be two distinct eigenvalues of $A$ with corresponding eigenvectors $v_1, v_2$. Then $v_1$ and $v_2$ are orthogonal.
[^rem9.2.5]: **Remark 9.2.5.** For general $n \times n$ (non-symmetric) matrices, the rank is $n$ minus the dimension of the nullspace, so it is $n$ minus the geometric multiplicity of $\lambda = 0$. Since symmetric matrices always have a complete set of eigenvalues and eigenvectors, the geometric multiplicities are always the same as the algebraic multiplicities.
[^cor9.2.4]: **Corollary 9.2.4.** The rank of a real symmetric matrix $A$ is the number of non-zero eigenvalues (counting repetitions).
[^thm9.2.1]: **Theorem 9.2.1 (Spectral Theorem).** Any symmetric matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ has $n$ real eigenvalues and an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$ consisting of its eigenvectors.
[^cor9.2.2]: **Corollary 9.2.2.** For any symmetric matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ there exists an orthogonal matrix $V \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ (whose columns are eigenvectors of $A$) such that $$A = V\Lambda V^\top,$$ where $\Lambda \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is a diagonal matrix with the eigenvalues of $A$ in its diagonal (and $V^\top V = I$).
[^rem9.2.3]: **Remark 9.2.3 (Eigendecomposition).** The decompositions in Corollary 9.2.2 and Theorem 9.1.1 are called Eigendecompositions.
[^prop9.2.6]: **Proposition 9.2.6.** Let $A$ be a real $n \times n$ symmetric matrix and let $v_1, \dots, v_n$ be an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of $A$ (the columns of the matrix $V$ in Corollary 9.2.2) and $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$ the associated eigenvalues. Then $$A = \sum_{k=1}^n \lambda_i v_i v_i^\top$$
[^prop9.2.10]: **Proposition 9.2.10 (Rayleigh Quotient).** Given a symmetric matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ the Rayleigh Quotient, defined for $x \in \mathbb{R}^n \setminus \{0\}$, as $$R(x) = \frac{x^\top A x}{x^\top x}$$ attains its maximum at $R(v_{\max}) = \lambda_{\max}$ and its minimum at $R(v_{\min}) = \lambda_{\min}$ where $\lambda_{\max}$ and $\lambda_{\min}$ are, respectively, the largest and smallest eigenvalues of $A$ and $v_{\max}, v_{\min}$ their associated eigenvectors.
[^def9.2.11]: **Definition 9.2.11.** A symmetric matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is said to be Positive Semidefinite (PSD) if all its eigenvalues are non-negative. If all the eigenvalues of $A$ are strictly positive then we say $A$ is Positive Definite (PD).
[^prop9.2.12]: **Proposition 9.2.12.** A symmetric matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is Positive Semidefinite if and only if $x^\top Ax \ge 0$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$. Analogously, a symmetric matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is Positive Definite if and only if $x^\top Ax > 0$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}^n \setminus \{0\}$.
[^def9.2.13]: **Definition 9.2.13 (Gram Matrix).** Given $n$ vectors, $v_1, \dots, v_n$ in $\mathbb{R}^m$ we call their Gram Matrix the $n \times n$ matrix of inner products $$G_{ij} = v_i^\top v_j.$$ If $V \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ is the matrix with columns $v_1, \dots, v_n$ in $\mathbb{R}^m$, then $G = V^\top V$ is the Gram matrix of $V$.
[^prop9.2.15]: **Proposition 9.2.15.** Given a real matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$, the non-zero eigenvalues of $A^\top A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ are the same as the ones of $AA^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$. Both matrices are symmetric and positive semidefinite.
[^prop9.2.16]: **Proposition 9.2.16. \[Cholesky decomposition]** Every symmetric positive semidefinite matrix $M$ is a Gram matrix of an upper triangular matrix $C$. $M = C^\top C$ is known as the Cholesky Decomposition.
[^rem9.2.14]: **Remark 9.2.14.** Given a matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$, as an abuse of notation, we sometimes also call $AA^\top$ a Gram matrix of $A$. Notice that, if $a_1, \dots, a_n \in \mathbb{R}^m$ are the columns of $A$ then $AA^\top$ is $m \times m$ and $$(38) \quad AA^\top = \sum_{i=1}^n a_i a_i^\top.$$
