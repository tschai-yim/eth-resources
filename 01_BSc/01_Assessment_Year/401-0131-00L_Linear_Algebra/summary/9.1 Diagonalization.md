## Diagonalizability & Eigenbases

- **Diagonalizable Matrix**: $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ diagonalizable $\iff \exists$ invertible $V$ s.t. $V^{-1}AV = \Lambda$ (diagonal). [^9.1.2]
- **Eigendecomposition Theorem**: $A$ has eigenvectors $v_1, \dots, v_n$ forming basis of $\mathbb{R}^n \iff A = V \Lambda V^{-1}$. [^9.1.1]
    - $V = [v_1 \dots v_n]$: Eigenvectors as columns.
    - $\Lambda$: Diagonal matrix with eigenvalues $\Lambda_{ii} = \lambda_i$.
    - **Interpretation**: Representation of linear transformation as scaling in eigenbasis.

## Conditions for Diagonalizability

- **Multiplicities**:
    - **Algebraic ($m_i$)**: Multiplicity of root $\lambda_i$ in characteristic polynomial $\det(A - \lambda I)$.
    - **Geometric**: Dimension of eigenspace $\dim(N(A - \lambda I))$. [^9.1.10]
- **Complete Set of Eigenvectors**:
    - Set of eigenvectors forming a basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$. [^9.1.3]
    - **Note**: Equivalent to $A$ being diagonalizable.
- **Criteria for Diagonalization**:
    - **General Criterion (Necessary & Sufficient)**: [^9.1.11]
        1. All eigenvalues are real.
        2. **Geometric Multiplicity = Algebraic Multiplicity** for *every* eigenvalue.
    - **Distinct Eigenvalues (Sufficient)**:
        - $n$ **distinct** real eigenvalues $\implies$ guaranteed diagonalizable.
        - *Reason*: Eigenvectors of distinct $\lambda$ are linearly independent.

## Matrix Classes & Examples

- **Diagonal Matrices**:
    - Canonical basis $e_1, \dots, e_n$ forms complete set. [^9.1.4]
- **Projection Matrices ($P$)**:
    - **Always diagonalizable**. [^9.1.6]
    - **Eigenvalues**: $0$ and $1$.
    - **Basis**: Union of basis for $U$ ($\lambda=1$) and $U^\perp$ ($\lambda=0$).
- **Non-Diagonalizable Examples**:
    - **Shear Matrix** $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$: [^9.1.9]
        - $\lambda = 0$ ($m=2$).
        - $\dim(N(A)) = 1$ (Geometric mult. $1 \neq 2$).
    - **Nilpotent Matrices**:
        - $A^k = 0$ for some $k$.
        - **Rule**: Only diagonalizable if $A=0$ (Zero matrix).
        - Non-zero nilpotent matrices are never diagonalizable (only eigenvalue is 0).

## Similarity Transformations

- **Definition**: $A, B$ **similar** if $B = S^{-1}AS$ for invertible $S$. [^9.1.7]
- **Properties**: [^9.1.8]
    - Same eigenvalues (identical characteristic polynomial).
    - $A$ diagonalizable $\iff B$ diagonalizable.
    - **Eigenvectors**: Transform via $S$ ($v_B = S^{-1}v_A$), not identical.

[^9.1.2]: **Definition 9.1.2 (Diagonalizable Matrix).** A matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is called a diagonalizable matrix if there exists an invertible matrix $V$ such that $V^{-1}AV = \Lambda$, where $\Lambda$ is a diagonal matrix.
[^9.1.1]: **Theorem 9.1.1.** Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$. Suppose that $A$ has eigenvectors $v_1, \dots, v_n \in \mathbb{R}^n$ that form a basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$. For $i \in \{1, \dots, n\}$ let $\lambda_i$ be the eigenvalue associated to $v_i$. Let $V$ be the matrix whose columns correspond to the eigenvectors $v_1, \dots, v_n$. Then, $$(35) \quad A = V \Lambda V^{-1},$$ where $\Lambda$ is a diagonal matrix with $\Lambda_{ii} = \lambda_i$ (and $\Lambda_{ij} = 0$ for all $i \neq j$).
[^9.1.10]: **Definition 9.1.10.** Given a matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ and an eigenvalue $\lambda$ of $A$ we call the dimension of $\mathbb{N}(A - \lambda I)$ the geometric multiplicity of $\lambda$.
[^9.1.3]: **Definition 9.1.3.** If, given a matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, we can build a basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$ with eigenvectors of $A$ we say that $A$ has a complete set of real eigenvectors.
[^9.1.11]: **Lemma 9.1.11.** A matrix has a complete set of real eigenvectors if all its eigenvalues are real and the geometric multiplicities are the same as the algebraic multiplicites of all its eigenvalues.
[^9.1.4]: **Example 9.1.4.** For $D \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ a diagonal matrix, the eigenvalues of $D$ are the diagonal entries of $D$. The canonical basis $e_1, \dots, e_n$ is a complete set of real eigenvectors of $D$.
[^9.1.6]: **Proposition 9.1.6 (Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors of a Projection Matrix).** Let $P$ be the projection matrix on the subspace $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$. Then $P$ has two eigenvalues, 0 and 1, and a complete set of real eigenvectors.
[^9.1.9]: **Example 9.1.9.** (a) The matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ does not have two linearly independent eigenvectors. Indeed, $\det(A - \lambda I) = \lambda^2$ which means that $\lambda = 0$ is the only eigenvalue and it has algebraic multiplicity 2. However, $N(A - 0I) = N(A)$ only has dimension 1. Hence, there is only one eigenvector (and multiples of it) corresponding to eigenvalue 0.
[^9.1.7]: **Definition 9.1.7 (Similar matrices).** We say that $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ and $B \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ are similar matrices if there exists an invertible matrix $S$ such that $B = S^{-1}AS$.
[^9.1.8]: **Proposition 9.1.8.** Similar matrices $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ and $B = S^{-1}AS \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ have the same eigenvalues. The matrix $A$ has a complete set of real eigenvectors if and only if $B$ does.
