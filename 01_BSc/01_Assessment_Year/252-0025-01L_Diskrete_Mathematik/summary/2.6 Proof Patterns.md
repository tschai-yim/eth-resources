- **Foundations:** Proof techniques are rigorous arguments justified by underlying tautologies, logical consequences, or equivalences.

## Proofs for Implications ($S \implies T$)

> **Definition 2.12.** The proof step of composing implications is as follows: If $S \implies T$ and $T \implies U$ are both true, then one concludes that $S \implies U$ is also true.

- **Composition of Implications (Transitivity):** Chain implications together.
    - **Justification:** Logical consequence in **Lemma 2.5**.

> **Lemma 2.5.** $(A \rightarrow B) \land (B \rightarrow C) \models A \rightarrow C$.

> **Definition 2.13.** A direct proof of an implication $S \implies T$ works by assuming $S$ and then proving $T$ under this assumption.

- **Direct Proof:**
    1. **Assume** premise $S$ is true.
    2. Logically derive that conclusion $T$ must be true.

> **Definition 2.14.** An indirect proof of an implication $S \implies T$ proceeds by assuming that $T$ is false and proving that $S$ is false, under this assumption.

- **Indirect Proof (Proof by Contrapositive):**
    1. **Assume** conclusion $T$ is false ($\neg T$).
    2. Show this assumption leads to the premise $S$ being false ($\neg S$).
    - **Justification:** $(\neg T \implies \neg S)$ is **logically equivalent** to $(S \implies T)$, per **Lemma 2.6**.

> **Lemma 2.6.** $\neg B \rightarrow \neg A \models A \rightarrow B$.

## Proofs for General Statements ($S$)

> **Definition 2.15.** A proof of a statement $S$ by use of the so-called modus ponens proceeds in three steps:
> 1. Find a suitable mathematical statement $R$.
> 2. Prove $R$.
> 3. Prove $R \implies S$.

- **Modus Ponens:** A foundational strategy.
    - **Justification:** Logical consequence in **Lemma 2.7**.

> **Lemma 2.7.** $A \land (A \rightarrow B) \models B$.

> **Definition 2.16.** A proof of a statement $S$ by case distinction proceeds in three steps:
> 4. Find a finite list $R_1, \dots, R_k$ of mathematical statements (the cases).
> 5. Prove that at least one of the $R_i$ is true (at least one case occurs).
> 6. Prove $R_i \implies S$ for $i=1, \dots, k$.

- **Case Distinction (Proof by Exhaustion):**
    1. Divide problem into exhaustive cases ($R_1 \lor R_2 \lor \dots \lor R_k$ must be true).
    2. Prove that $S$ holds in every single case.
    - **Justification:** Logical consequence in **Lemma 2.8**.

> **Lemma 2.8.** For every k we have
> $(A_1 \lor \dots \lor A_k) \land (A_1 \rightarrow B) \land \dots \land (A_k \rightarrow B) \models B$.

> **Definition 2.17.** A proof by contradiction of a statement $S$ proceeds in three steps:
> 7. Find a suitable mathematical statement $T$.
> 8. Prove that $T$ is false.
> 9. Assume that $S$ is false and prove (from this assumption) that $T$ is true (a contradiction).

- **Proof by Contradiction:**
    1. To prove $S$, **assume** it is false ($\neg S$).
    2. Show this assumption leads to a logical **contradiction** (deriving a known falsehood).
    3. Conclude the assumption ($\neg S$) was wrong, so $S$ must be true.
    - **Example:** Proof that $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational assumes it is rational, leading to the contradiction that a fully reduced fraction $m/n$ has both $m$ and $n$ being even.
    - **Justification:** Logical consequence in **Lemma 2.9**.

> **Lemma 2.9.** $(\neg A \rightarrow B) \land \neg B \models A$.

## Proofs involving Quantifiers & Sets

> **Definition 2.18.** Consider a universe $X$ of parameters and consider for each $x$ in $X$ a statement, denoted $S_x$. An existence proof is a proof of the statement that $S_x$ is true for at least one $x \in X$. An existence proof is constructive if it exhibits an $a$ for which $S_a$ is true, and otherwise it is non-constructive.

- **Existence Proofs:** Prove statements of the form $∃x P(x)$.
    - **Constructive:** Provide a specific example.
    - **Non-constructive:** Show an example must exist without identifying it.
        - Example: Proving there are infinitely many primes by showing that for any number $m$, a prime must exist between $m$ and $m!+1$.

> **Theorem 2.10.** If a set of $n$ objects is partitioned into $k < n$ sets, then at least one of these sets contains at least $\lceil n/k \rceil$ objects.

- **Pigeonhole Principle:** A tool for non-constructive existence proofs. If $n$ "pigeons" are in $k$ "pigeonholes" and $n>k$, one hole must have $>1$ pigeon.

> **Definition 2.19.** Consider a universe $X$ of parameters and consider for each $x$ in $X$ a statement, denoted $S_x$. A proof by counterexample is a proof of the statement that $S_x$ is not true for all $x \in X$, by exhibiting an $a$ (called counterexample) such that $S_a$ is false.

- **Proof by Counterexample:** **Disproves** a universal statement ($∀x P(x)$).
    - This is a **constructive existence proof** for the negation $∃x ¬P(x)$.
    - Requires finding a single instance where the statement fails.

## Proof by Induction (for statements over $\mathbb{N}$)

- **Principle:** Proves statements of the form $∀n \in \mathbb{N}: P(n)$.
    1. **Basis Step:** Prove the statement for the first case (e.g., $P(0)$).
    2. **Induction Step:** Prove that **if** the statement holds for an arbitrary $n$ (**induction hypothesis**), **then** it must also hold for $n+1$. (Prove $P(n) \implies P(n+1)$).
- **Justification:** Based on the structure of natural numbers, formalized in **Theorem 2.11**.

> **Theorem 2.11.** For the universe $\mathbb{N}$ and an arbitrary unary predicate $P$ we have
> $P(0) \land ∀n (P(n) \rightarrow P(n+1)) \implies ∀n P(n)$.
